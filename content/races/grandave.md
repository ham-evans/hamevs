## Preface

I have been told that I had a quite interesting life and that I should write down some of it for future generations that might be interested. Initially I had great ambitions to write things in some detail however despite several starts I never got around to really finishing. This time I decided to just hit the highlights and do it by dictating using NaturallySpeaking. In these days of short attention spans highlights only are probably the right thing to do anyway. I am afraid it will be chopped up a bit but bear with me please.

In this document I have focused almost exclusively on events that I directly related to that might not be evident otherwise. Along the way I got married, had three fine children (who are now adults) and have been blessed with eight handsome, bright grandchildren. Perhaps I should have included more about these very important aspects of my life; however I decided to reduce the focus in order to actually finish this synoptic!

## The Early Years

**Some Background:** From October, 1929 until the 30s decade the country was mired in what was later called "the great depression". This led to widespread poverty and many families had to struggle to survive. The president was Herbert Hoover, who was replaced by Franklin Roosevelt in the 1932 election.

**Clovis, New Mexico.** I was born on December 28, 1930 in a small home in a residential section of Clovis. I was named "David Donald Evans," however my Family just called me Donnie (and later, Don) I guess since that convention was commonly used by folks with a Southern (Texas) background. At that time our family consisted of two children by my father from a previous marriage (his first wife had died from the flu during the 1918 epidemic), two sisters, Lenora and DeAun, and a brother, Carroll. Finding employment was difficult and the wages were also quite low. We were poor as were many families that lived around us I was told.

**Farmington, New Mexico:** In 1934 the family moved to Farmington, NM, a small farming community. For a while we lived in a small home near the downtown however I have no recollection of that. We later moved to what we called the Calloway place, located well over a mile out on E. Main St. There was a large house and there was a fairly large farm there as well, along with the mandatory outhouse. We were still quite poor, as were many neighbors as the result of the depression. Dad had arranged for us to live in the house and farm the land in exchange for allowing the former owner to live in a small cottage on the property. As was typical in those days the farm provided milk from cows, some meat from the hogs and chickens, and plenty of vegetables and fruits from the land. Thus we were able to live pretty much "off the land," needing to purchase only staples such as sugar, salt, etc., and of course clothing when hand-me-downs weren't available. Dad repaired our shoes with half-sole kits and Mom canned heavily during the growing season.

This property was over a mile from the schools however we were just under the distance that allowed use of school buses. Hence we had to walk to school daily, even when the snow was up to our waist! (An exaggeration of course.)

Next we moved closer to town to a property that I best remember since we lived there from the time I was about eight years old until I went away to college. The address was 620 E. Main St. It had a gravel road running in front of the house that was the main road from Farmington to Aztec and on to Durango Colorado. We didn't have indoor plumbing but had to use an outhouse for bathroom needs, which was really fun during cold, snowy weather!

This property had about an acre of land with a portion of it across the street, where we kept a cow. The ample land beyond the back yard provided us with fruits and vegetables, even enough for canning. There was a chicken pen in the backyard so we had eggs and fried chicken to eat as well. With no indoor plumbing water had to be heated on top of a large coal fired range in the kitchen. Water was provided with an underground reservoir (called a cistern). We refilled it by way of a ditch which brought in irrigation water. The water was passed through gravel and sand filters to remove debris. It was then treated with chlorine to kill the germs. When one needed water for any reason one used a hand pump located over the cistern in the back porch area. A once a week bath in a large wash tub in the warm kitchen was the rule.

As a child I remember playing "cars" in the backyard with friends and often with my brother Carroll. In the evening we would play outdoors until dark and our games would often be "hide and seek" or other sorts of multiple player games. (Realize of course that we had no video games nor television nor cell phones so we pretty much had to entertain ourselves.) We would also listen to some of our favorite radio programs including Fibber Magee and Molly, Jack Benny, and others similar to these. From this East Main location we could easily walk to school and even walk back for lunch if we wished.

**Premature Death of My Father.** Just days before my 10th birthday my father, Charles Hamilton Evans, died on Christmas Day, 1940. He suffered a fatal heart attack at the age of 54. My younger brother, Gary, was only 18 months old at that time. Thus my mother was faced with raising a baby, a 10-year-old, and a 15 year old (Carroll). She had never worked outside the home, which was common at that time. At the time I didn't realize how poor we were. Fortunately my older sister, DeAun, who was 19, was able to help support the family, as did an older half-brother, Charles Jr., who was an attorney in Denver. Mother got a job at the school cafeteria part-time and DeAun worked at the local telephone exchange. I did odd jobs, including delivering telegrams to the local residents, riding a bicycle to wherever I needed to go.

Mother had two sisters that lived in Albuquerque, both of which had families as well. I spent a portion of one summer with my cousins in Albuquerque and unfortunately came down with what was diagnosed as scarlet fever. The local doctor concluded that I suffered from some heart valve damage, a known condition as result of this disease. For this reason I was never able to participate in high school sports although I supported the teams by serving as team manager for both basketball and football and announced games and this sort of thing.

**World War II.** Our country entered the war after the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941 and the war did not end until August, 1945 immediately after use of the first atomic bomb. As an impressionable child and teenager I was keenly interested in the progress of the war and avidly read about it in the Albuquerque Journal and the "Weekly Reader," furnished by the school system. I also followed developments on the radio. I was particularly interested in aviation and I can recall reading (and rereading) a small paperback entitled "30 seconds over Tokyo," describing the Doolittle raid on Tokyo in 1942. This interest in aviation let me down another path which I will describe later.

I was also a Boy Scout, eventually becoming a Life Scout. The emphasis at that time was on outdoor activities, including camping and hiking. When I became older I joined the Air Scouts, a group whose interest obviously was in aviation. One of the activities was a one-week camp hosted by the Air Force in Albuquerque. We reviewed military aviation and took classes and this sort of thing. There was also a friendly competition and I was able to win eight hours of instruction, which I subsequently took in a light plane at the Farmington airport. This was great fun but it just made me want to fly even more.

Mother remarried in September of 1942. My stepfather's name was Walter L Owens although he went by his initials, WL. Mr. Owens reopened a small combination market and filling station a few months after he and Mother were married. I worked for him stocking shelves, selling meat at the meat counter, pumping gasoline, and cutting and delivering large pieces of ice to our customers. I remember that milk was 13 cents per quart! This was during World War II of course so most purchased foods were rationed as was gasoline. Candy was difficult to get, but the store was able to receive some which was sold to our best customers only. Among other things we saw the first use of oleomargarine as a substitute for butter. WL died in 1947 of a cerebral hemorrhage.

Later I worked in a drugstore while I was going to high school. Initially I worked at the soda fountain but then moved to working in the pharmacy department, eventually filling prescriptions (which were of course carefully checked by the pharmacist). I was a good student in high school and very much enjoyed science and mathematics courses. My favorite courses were geometry and algebra. I was also active in extracurricular activities such as band (I played the drums in the band and in the orchestra) as well as the science club. I was elected student body president in my senior year (1949). Our school was small; our senior class consisted of 53 graduates.

## College Days & Beyond

**Perspective of the 50's:** The nation became involved in the Korean War which started in June of 1950 involving the conflict between communist and non-Communist portions of Korea. This is also the decade in which civil unrest led to the ruling that segregation was illegal in the United States. The space age also began when the Russians launched of the first Earth satellite in 1957 (Sputnik).

**University of New Mexico.** After graduation from high school in 1949 I enrolled in the University of New Mexico, located in Albuquerque. I had earned a tuition scholarship to the University as result of my good grades in high school. What really helped was an invitation from my two Aunts and Uncles (Mother's sisters) in Albuquerque to provide my room and board for the first year. This arrangement basically enabled me to start college. I was able to get a part-time job in a local drugstore by virtue of my experience at the Farmington drug store. I typically worked after classes most days as well as weekends. Fortunately I was able to take the bus both to and from school as well as to and from work locations. I was forever appreciative toward the W Rue Goodrums and Ralph Loyds for providing this assistance. I was later able to live in a dormitory on campus since I was able to save enough money while working in the summers. My family was unable to provide any financial assistance.

At UNM I began taking courses in the Pharmacy department, although for the first year they mostly involved general subjects with an emphasis on chemistry and physics. I also joined the Air Force ROTC (Reserve Officers Training Corps) to further pursue my interest in military aviation and to take advantage of their offer to provide modest monthly cash payments for the last two years of the program.

I continued working during the summers, doing a variety of jobs and continued working a pretty full schedule during every school year. Among other things I spent one summer selling aluminum pots and pans (really!), one summer working on a surveying crew, and another working in a local clothing store. At school I continued working at a pharmacy, although during my senior year I got a good job working overnight as a watchman and wrecker dispatcher at an Albuquerque Chevrolet dealer. I got a lot of studying done, but was pretty tired the following day from the lack of sleep.

After my freshman year I switched my major to chemistry since this was my real love at the time. I wasn't really sure what I would do with chemistry after graduation but I knew that I would be expected to serve in the Air Force for at least two years to fulfill my ROTC obligation. I did well in chemistry and especially enjoyed organic chemistry. With help from my instructor I worked on an honors project during my senior year. This involved chemically synthesizing a complex cyclic amine which was believed to cause cancer (it was a product of cigarette smoke). Finishing this synthesis required working in the Laboratory during the Christmas Break! As a result of my good grades and my work on the honors project I graduated with a BS with Honors in Chemistry in June, 1953.

I was also active in student government serving on both the publications committee and the student standards committee. I was also the campaign manager for Al Utton, a friend from Aztec New Mexico, who was running for student body president. Al won that race and went on to be selected as a Rhodes Scholar for study at Oxford University in England.

During my senior year I was selected to be the Cadet Wing Commander (as the Cadet Colonel) of the Air Force ROTC unit at the University. The Cadet Col. is the senior cadet officer of the unit and is the student commander of the 700 cadets. I felt especially proud to wear that uniform on campus with my many-striped shoulder boards.

Toward the end of my senior year the Air Force Colonel, the commander of the ROTC unit, recommended that I take a physical examination to see if I was qualified to undergo flight training. I informed him that I sincerely doubted that I would pass the physical due to my heart defect (which was result of the scarlet fever bout described earlier). Much to my surprise I passed this physical and I was delighted to be able enter pilot training subsequent to my graduation from the University.

## Air Force Duty

In September, 1953, I received orders to report to Lackland Air Force Base in San Antonio Texas for processing into pilot training. We spent a few days at Lackland undergoing another physical and some orientation classes. Finally my orders came through to report to Spence Field, Moultrie, Georgia to begin flight training. This was my first exposure to the deep South and I was shocked at the extent of the terrible discrimination, which was new to me.

The flying training was intense, but I became friends with several class members and overall it was a fun experience. We typically spent a half day in classes and the other half day in the actual flying training, initially with an instructor of course. We first flew Piper Super Cubs to become oriented to flying. My previous training helped so I was able to easily adapt and to solo this small plane.

Next we flew a larger heavier aircraft that had served this purpose through many a class during World War II. It was the T-6, a low wing conventional landing gear (tail wheel) aircraft. It had a lot of power and was difficult to land properly because of the low wings and limited visibility forward. We were expected to do a power-off approach and execute a 3-point landing just at the stalling speed, which is quite difficult to learn. A few members of our class had minor accidents when landing, typically dragging a wing tip, however there were no injuries. Of course several of my classmates "washed out" of training. I had a great instructor, Mr. Levy, so that made things easier and a lot more fun.

![T-6 flight training](/races/grandave-1.jpg)

Perhaps the scariest part of training was during our night flying cross-country trips when we were required to fly over the Georgia Okefenokee Swamp which we knew was teeming with snakes and alligators! The engine always seemed to run rough during these flights, but of course it was just our imagination. I completed this "basic" training course in May, 1954 and was selected for single-engine training for the next phase (much to my delight). Several of my classmates were assigned to multi-engine training. The rest of us received orders to go to Laredo Air Force base in Texas for the next phase: instruction in the T-34 aircraft prior to transitioning into jets!

The T-34 airplane was a tricycle-gear bird and was very easy to fly, to do acrobats, and to learn formation flying. After about 40 hours in this airplane we were ready for jet trainers. The airplane is used at that time was the T-33, which again had been used for some time for this purpose. It took some getting used to because of the amount of power, the speed, and especially the landing. One had to make a very low approach to the runway at 120 knots (140 mph) before reducing speed for landing. Also there was a significant delay in power as the turbine speed wound up making aborted landings exciting. However once these things were mastered it was a pleasure to fly. We especially had fun in practicing mock dog-fighting, desperately trying to get a (pretend) shot at a classmate, who is likewise trying to out-maneuver you.

Laredo is based in South Texas and gets hot there during the summer. Temperatures on the hot asphalt out on the flight line hovered near 140°. Taxiing out was stressful since one was strapped into an oven-like vehicle with a tight rubber face mask irritating your shaving stubble. It was a relief to finally take off however since the cockpit was equipped with an efficient cooler that blew tiny snowballs over your shoulder as you accelerated on the runway! We got to take extended cross country trips with an instructor and view the countryside from 40,000 feet. Formation flying was stressed since most flying during subsequent active duty will require good skills in staying close to the leader.

I had a little fun during the last phase of this training. I went on a routine solo training flight to put in some time and practice some maneuvers, etc. Instead of practicing I flew directly to Weslaco, Texas, where a sister (LeeElla) and her husband lived. I proceeded to give the town a good buzzing by diving low, and doing rolls while I pulled up. My sister said everyone was talking about it later. Her husband, George Lancaster, was a physician. He said that he treated a small boy who raced outside to see what was happening and cut his foot on a piece of glass. Fortunately no one reported the buzzing to the Air Force since it was very much against regulations.

At the completion of this phase of training we received our coveted silver wings! This was October, 1954. However we now had to make a significant decision. If one agreed to serve for an additional five years in the Air Force, then you could attend advanced training in fighter tactics training at Nellis Air Force Base in Nevada. In reality if one extended their tour then it would mean 7 years in the Air Force, in which case one may as well stay for another 20 or so. In other words it was likely a career choice. It was a tough decision, but I decided to just serve my remaining time with an option to get out of Air Force at the conclusion of my 3-year tour. When I subsequently received my orders I found that I had been assigned to armament systems officer training at Lowry Air Force Base in Denver, Colorado.

This 6-month training was intense, focusing on instruction in electronics (for overseeing maintenance of electronic gun sights, as well as instruction in 50 caliber and 20 mm guns, as well as conventional bombs. We had 3 T-33's assigned so that those of us that were pilots could maintain flight proficiency by getting at least 4 hours of flying time per month which kept one eligible for an additional $100 per month allowance. Flying over the Rocky Mountains near Denver was delightful. This course was completed in February, 1955.

When my orders for the next assignment arrived I was surprised (and disappointed) to find that I had been assigned to Cannon Air Force Base in Clovis, New Mexico. My disappointment was because I was familiar with New Mexico, having been born in Clovis, lived in Farmington, and gone to NM University in Albuquerque. I had hoped to be assigned to a different location where I could get a broader view. Also, there is very little in eastern New Mexico that would appeal to a young, single male. It is literally "in the middle of nowhere" as they say.

Cannon Air Force Base housed a fighter bomber group of the H model of the F-86 aircraft. When I reported in I was informed that I was going to become the armament system officer for the 474th fighter bomber group, consisting of three squadrons, each with approximately 30 aircraft. Our primary mission was delivery of small atomic weapons! As soon as my top secret clearance was approved I was sent back to Denver for a course in handling and preparing atomic bombs for flight. It was quite interesting as well as disturbing when one realized how terrible those things were.

Since this was a fighter base we had several T-33 aircraft available to maintain pilot proficiency. This allowed me to check out an aircraft for a cross country flight on weekends, allowing me to make several trips to Nellis Air Force Base near Las Vegas. At that time my Mother and Stepfather, Mr. Selby lived in Las Vegas.

Living in Clovis and flying out of Cannon Air Force base was a challenge at times due to the severe dust storms that plagued West Texas and Eastern New Mexico. Our bachelor quarters were in old WWII barracks that leaked dust terribly and landing during one of these dust spells was difficult because of the reduced visibility and high winds!

I had a disturbing incident during a cross country flight from Clovis to Robins Air Force base in Georgia. I was accompanied by an Air Force Captain (I was a 1st Lt. at that time), although he was along only for the ride to visit some of his family. We unfortunately flew through a severe weather area that was pocketed with thunderstorms. Needless to say it was scary when everything got black, except for lightning flashes. Then the aircraft was buffeted and tossed first up and then down by strong up and down drafts. It made a roller coaster ride seem mild! We finally reached clear air on the other side about the time we were leaving Texas.

Then the engine failed, turning our plane into a very heavy glider. I tried restarting without success so we started looking for landing sites that were within range, the alternative being to bail out. We determined that Barksdale Air Force base in Louisiana was within range so we headed in that direction. We had been required to practice engine-out procedures and engine-out landings during training so it looked doable with a little luck. I declared an emergency so we were given priority to enter the pattern and land, which was accomplished without incident (except for heavy breathing and sweating by both of us).

As my tour neared an end in August, 1956, my Commander, Col. Scott, tried very hard to get me to stay in the Air Force. As an inducement they allowed me to check out and fly the F-86H, a really hot bird at that time. The ground crew all come out to see all first-time pilots land an F-86 since new pilots often blow out a tire from braking too abruptly. I was well aware of this so made a very careful landing roll-out, much to their disappointment. I enjoyed this experience and got in about 12 hours in the F-86 before being discharged from active duty.

I had earlier decided to go back to school for another degree. Being eligible for Veteran's benefits (tuition plus housing allowance) to help pay the way was also a consideration.

## University of Colorado (1956–1958)

I enrolled in the aeronautical engineering department since I thought my flight experience would be helpful and I also wished to stay in the aeronautical field. It turned out that one of my good friends from the Air Force duty had gone into a partnership and was operating the small fixed base airport at Boulder Colorado. I became a flight instructor teaching various folks including some students how to fly a light aircraft. Of course I had to work in the flight instructor's duties between classes or on afternoons when I didn't have classes. I was fortunate to have earned my instructor rating as well an instrument rating based on my Air Force credentials. I had some interesting times instructing students in a variety of small aircraft as well as some exciting times including taking off in deep snow or landing during very strong thunderstorm winds. And of course approving someone for a solo flight, and then sweating out their first landings was always a bit stressful!

The courses I took included engineering drawing (now obsolete of course), metallurgy, strength of materials, and several specialty courses in the aeronautics department including airplane design, propulsion, aerodynamics and this sort of thing. Our handy slide rules received a lot of use, although the first hand-held scientific calculator had just been released by Texas Instruments Company, but the $400 price was daunting.

We had just begun studying the application of our specialty for space purposes since the early work on space projects had just started. I took a course in jet propulsion and did a senior project on a manned earth spacecraft and reentry vehicle.

I did well in both my aero courses as well as those in other departments. I think I was better focused as a result of my Air Force duty. At any rate I was surprised and pleased when the faculty selected me as the Outstanding Aeronautics Graduate for the class of 1958. My combination of BS degrees (chemistry and aeronautical engineering) provided a good background for finding a job. I received several offers, including one from Boeing in Seattle, but was quite interested in the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena. They had just built the upper stage for our first earth satellite, Explorer 1.

I was invited to visit JPL to meet the staff and for them to meet me. I was impressed with JPL during my one-day visit. It was a small firm at that time, with about 2000 employees. After my visit I received an offer from JPL as a Research Engineer in the Propulsion Research Section at a starting salary of $550/month (a lot of money at that time). The opportunity to work at the beginnings of the space program was also a major factor in accepting this offer. I was also interested in working in Southern California.

## Jet Propulsion Laboratory (1958–1994)

My first job was with a small Group doing research on monopropellants to furnish the propulsive thrust needed to trim up the trajectory for our early spacecraft. I initially worked for Donald Lee, who subsequently became a very good family friend. We worked on the catalytic decomposition of hydrazine (N2H4) to power small, 50-lb thrusters on spacecraft headed for the moon or one of the planets. Our design was subsequently used on several spacecraft.

During this period we used a group of data analysts consisting of an all-female staff located in the same building. They would utilize mechanical calculators and do hand plots of things like rocket engine chamber pressure vs run time, etc. This is where I met my future wife Nancy Hayes! We were married on February 20, 1960.

Next I worked on advanced monopropellants consisting of a strong oxidizer with a dissolved organic fuel. The most successful candidate was one called Cavea-B. The only real difficulty was that it was so unstable that if it didn't ignite properly, and subsequently exploded in the rocket engine, then the explosion propagated up the fuel line until stopped by a detonation trap, hopefully before reaching the fuel supply tank. I should hasten to add that during all of our testing we were well protected by very thick concrete walls, depending upon high-speed photography and instrumentation to record the needed data. After working on various methods of assuring a successful ignition we eventually concluded that this compound was too dangerous for use as a rocket propellant.

After my good friend Don Lee left JPL, I was asked to support our first attempts to reach the moon with a spacecraft. I was the responsible propulsion engineer on the midcourse propulsion systems used on the Ranger-6, 7, and 8 spacecraft. This involved development and testing of the propulsion system consisting of a small 50-lb hydrazine thruster and its associated propellant tank, valves, etc. As part of this assignment I went to Cape Canaveral along with Nancy and our first son, the toddler Tim. I was responsible for supervising the propellant loading and supporting both the launch and flight operations of each spacecraft. The first of these, Ranger 6, was unsuccessful when the TV system failed to come on as it approached the moon. However my propulsion system worked flawlessly.

The next spacecraft, Ranger 7, was successful and took the first image of the Moon by a U.S. spacecraft, on 31 July 1964. It was followed by Ranger 8, also successful.

![Ranger 7 - First image of the Moon by a U.S. spacecraft](/races/grandave-2.jpg)

In 1966 I was appointed as Supervisor of the Combustion Devices Group, consisting of 6 to 8 engineers and several technicians. Our work focused on small rocket engines using advanced bipropellants, including hydrazine and Nitrogen Tetroxide, hydrazine and Liquid Fluorine (nasty stuff). Most of the testing of these propellants was moved from Pasadena to our Edwards Test Station, adjacent to the north runway of Edwards Air Force Base. I visited our test station regularly to witness key tests from a remote blockhouse. A highlight was being able to watch the U-2 aircraft take off, with its very, very wide wingspan.

In 1969 we reorganized again. I was appointed supervisor of the Flight Systems Development group as well as Assistant Section Manager. The group's role was supporting the development of propulsion systems for an expanding planetary exploration program. Prior to this reorganization I had been appointed as the responsible engineer for the propulsion system on Mariner 9. Mariner 9 was to be the first spacecraft that would orbit another planet, in this case Mars. It was to use a bipropellant system and a 100 lb rocket engine with a dual role. It was to fire for a very short time to make course corrections en route to Mars however the primary function was to fire for a very long time to put the spacecraft in orbit about Mars.
