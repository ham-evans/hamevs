My given name was Nancy Lou Hayes. When I married I dropped my middle name and my legal name is now Nancy Hayes Evans.

I was born on October 29th 1933 in Southern California in the city of Altadena which is adjacent to Pasadena where I grew up and lived until I married.

I have two sisters. Barbara Ann and Marilyn Mae.

All my parents, grandparents and great-grandparents were born in the USA. My fathers family came originally from Scotland, England and Germany. Some of my mother's mothers family came from Ireland but we have not been able to find out about her fathers side of the family.

## The Neighborhood

I lived in Pasadena CA in a house that my father's father built at 427 Virginia Ave. The house is still there today looking essentially as it did when I was little.

I'm not sure just what the boundaries of Lamanda Park were but my little section of it was a four block strip bounded on the south by San Pasqual, on the north by Colorado Boulevard, on the west by Santa Anita and Virginia Ave. on the east. I was pretty much allowed to roam this area going farther and farther as I grew older. All the blocks were boarded by sidewalks which I used for riding my tricycle, first just on Virginia Ave., then around the whole block. Later skates became the vehicle and scooters made of two by fours with old skate wheels screwed on the bottom. There were vacant lots at both corners of Virginia and the dirt from them was always finding it's way on to the sidewalk and causing terrible spills that would rip off many layers of skin from knees and elbows. I spent years with various stages of scabs on one or more of those vulnerable areas. One time I had a terrible fall while wearing my new brown and tan reversible raincoat and tore out the elbows through both layers of fabric. I was really scared to show my mom what had happened because I knew she'd be really mad, but for some reason she wasn't. Maybe the genuine tears I was shedding softened her heart.

The library was just a couple of blocks away and I walked up there a lot, especially during the summer months. There was, for many years, only one other kid on my block of Virginia Ave., so I had to entertain myself a lot of the time and I mostly did that by reading. I'd go to the library every other day or so to get an arm load of books and then go home and read one after the other. I wish I'd kept a list of what I read as I do now. I think that would be an interesting picture of the times. The ones I do remember were Nancy Drew, the Hardy Boys.

The front yard was dominated by a huge eucalyptus tree that had been planted by Grandpa Hayes and several magnolia trees that were planted in the parking — the small strip of grass between the street and the sidewalk. In later years the roots of the magnolias ripped up the sidewalk and now it is a mishmash of raised concrete and asphalt. A lovely granite and glass street light was between the two magnolias and served as home base when we kids played hide and go seek. The area along the front porch was planted with gardenias and hibiscus which in the summer were a mass of blooms, creating a colorful and fragrant entrance and there were always bowls of them inside the house. The beautiful and fragrant, or smelly — depending on your nose, magnolia blossoms were also cut and put inside. The brown stems that fell from the flowers left on the tree and the small yellow stamens with a red tip that fell from the blossom turned into pretend cigarettes and matches for us kids. No one in our family smoked that I know of but somehow we knew it was supposed to make you look grownup and glamorous or perhaps, since we played cowboys and Indians a lot, it was to look tough.

There was a long driveway from the street to the garage, and when I was old enough it was my job to wash down the driveway, the front porch and the side walk. I loved doing that, playing along barefooted in the water and daydreaming at the same time. The front porch was unusually big, about seven by ten feet, and was raised above the ground a couple of feet. It was made of concrete and when the hose was allowed to run on it, it became very slippery and you could "skate" around on it with bare feet. Once in a while you ended up on your bottom but it was still fun.

Strange as it may seem, that old place was the scene of two of my favorite activities as a little kid. The first involved the garage doors which were made of broad wooden boards and opened out as a barn door does, the paint was yellow faded to a cream color. If water got on the old paint it made it look much brighter and one of the things I was allowed to do was "paint" those doors using water and a big ole house painter's brush. I must have spent hours, climbing up and down a ladder, giving those doors a coat of quick drying paint and I felt just like I really was painting them as I watched the paint seem to become new. Just inside the garage on one of the walls was a very large chalk board that was my other special interest. It must have been at least four feet long and three and a half tall but set low enough that I could reach all of it. The thing I liked most to draw was a great big peach tree with many, many branches and every branch covered with loads of tin pink peach blossoms. I was very proud of myself that I could make such a beautiful tree.

Of course, from time to time we had to clean out the garage and that was a Saturday chore that took most of the day and involved all the family. Everything came out on to the driveway and was dusted off, the inside was swept in every nook and cranny to get rid of dirt and cobwebs and then it was all put back, neatly rearranged. It was hard work, and dirty, but I never minded because seeing it all clean and straightened up made it seem worth the effort. There was a resident Black Widow spider that lived in a little niche by the light switch. As I recall we never did anything about it, except to be careful where we put our fingers.

## Childhood

When I was a child life was much simpler than it is today. There were way fewer choices of what you could do, where you could go and what you could eat or wear. A lot of my clothes were homemade dresses or hand knitted sweaters. I walked, skated or biked to school, played kick the can, hide and seek, dodgeball and tetherball. I also played marbles with the boys while the girls jumped rope, a skill, which along with doing tricks on the bars, I did not have. I was a Brownie and a Girl Scout. We did not travel much but sometimes took the trolley car to Los Angeles or took a Sunday drive to another town or to the beach. Movies were a fairly often Saturday entertainment — No TV in those days. One thing the we kids missed was Bubble Gum. One in a great while a little shop in our neighborhood would get some and when we heard about it we would rush to line up at the store and we could buy one piece of Double Bubble Bubble Gum.

## Divorce

My mother and father were divorced when I was about eight or so and I have very few recollections of the time when he was still at home. I do remember that on some hot summer evenings he would drive us girls to a stand a few blocks from our house that sold orange juice poured over finely shaved ice. That was such a treat! I recall that he took me to school one morning and left me alone on the playground not knowing that it was a school holiday. Obviously my mother didn't know that either. Someone rescued me. Only one time do I remember hearing the two of them fight. I was playing paper dolls in the living room and he came and slammed closed the pocket doors that separated the livingroom and dining room. The paper doll that I was playing with was very special to me. It was a very big baby doll, with a big round head and a fat baby body and it had lots of clothes that someone had cut out for me. I think I was frightened that day. And I remember the day he left, him standing at the front door and saying goodbye to my mother, "Goodbye, Delt," and then walking off. I don't know what anyone else felt that day but I certainly don't recall any traumatic feeling on my part. Maybe I just don't remember or perhaps it was because my dad really hadn't been around that much anyway. He left our family to marry another woman.

## Food

We ate dinner in the dining room with my father at one end of the table, near the kitchen, my mother at the other and the girls on either side. There was a swinging door between the kitchen and dining room so things could be carried easily from one room to the other. A few things that were served rather regularly I remember well. Carrot and raisin salad — grated carrots mixed with a few raisins and mayonnaise or Miracle Whip, scooped into a half ball with an ice cream scoop and served on a small salad plate with a lettuce leaf. Canned fruit salad served in a small bowl with, if you were lucky, one of the few half cherries in the can. I don't know what the big deal about the cherry was because all the fruit tasted pretty much the same. A much less enjoyed salad, but often served for company, was tomato aspic, a molded salad made with tomatoes and/or tomato juice, celery, bell pepper and gelatin. It was made in individual molds, which I still have, and served quivering on a plate with lettuce leaf.

Mock turkey was one of my very favorite dishes. It was made with canned tuna, crushed potato chips and canned mushroom soup all mixed together and baked. I could hardly get enough of it. Meatloaf was a standard and seemed to taste so much better than it does these days. We ate a wide variety of meats, or at least different parts of the cow. Pan fried liver and onions was a weekly meal, especially when I was older. The left over liver was ground in a meat grinder, mixed with chopped onion, salt, pepper and mayonnaise and used as a very tasty sandwich spread. We called it liverwurst, but it was not at all like the liverwurst that you buy at the meat market. Tongue was a great favorite of mine, cooked whole in the pressure cooker or simmered for hours with peppercorns and onions and then skinned and sliced for serving. It was sometimes served with a sauce of tomatoes and peas but I preferred it plain. I loved having leftover tongue for lunch, just warmed in it's juice or on a sandwich. Occasionally we had roasted or braised heart, it was rather tough and strong tasting and was probably cheap. I loved fried rabbit, it is similar to chicken in texture but with it's unique own taste. It was common in the meat market.

For desserts, Jello with or without fruit, custards and tapioca pudding were common. Chocolate pudding, made from scratch or later a Jello pudding mix, was more of a treat and so creamy and delicious it was hard to get enough. Most things that were baked or cooked at home in those days were made with fresh, full strength ingredients, whole milk, cream, real butter and whole eggs. All those foods contributed to the rich flavors and smooth textures of the final product.

## Diseases

Almost every schoolchild, including me, got the measles, mumps and or the chicken pox. Polio was a major worry, but the only way it affected me was that it limited what I was allowed to do. It was thought that swimming pools and large crowds were places where it might be spread so I could not go to a public pool or to any large events but I rarely would have done either of those anyway. The introduction of penicillin in the 1940's was a huge step in the treatment of common infections which did help me once or twice.

## World War II

The only war that had a direct impact on my life was WWII. I had just turned eight years old when Pearl Harbor was bombed on Dec 7, 1941 and I remember well my folks listening to reports of the attack on the radio and saying we would be going to war with Japan. I had no idea of what that actually meant and never really understood all the dangers. There were family members and friends who were in the armed forces fighting overseas but fortunately all survived the war. At home we collected scrap metal, tin foil, rubber, used cooking fat and old newspaper for use in making things needed in the war. Gasoline and tires were rationed, each family allowed only a small amount so driving was limited. The same with some foods, sugar, meat, coffee, butter all in short supply. That was when oleomargarine was introduced to replace butter. These were all small inconveniences. At school we took our pennies, nickels and dimes and bought war stamps to raise money for the war. There were airplane plants in several places along the California coast which could have been targets for enemy planes and because of that we had to black out all our windows at night and no street lamps or other outside light were on so that there was no way the enemy could know where they were. The Vietnam war, because it was the first war shown on television, gave us all a look at the horrors of war which certainly did influence my view and feeling of war in general.

## School

Pasadena was considered to have a rather good school program. It was organized on a unique system of grade grouping called the six, four, four plan. The elementary grades were Kindergarten through the sixth grade, the junior high grades were seven through ten which were located together at one facility and the last two years of high school were taken at one of the city colleges along with the first two years of college if you did not go to a four year college. Formal graduations took place at the end of the tenth grade, again at the end of high school and, for those who stayed on, at the completion of the first two years of college level courses. The graduations themselves were quite spectacular. All the graduates took part in one huge, colorful, and at the time quite thrilling, ceremony that was held in the Rose Bowl. and included a grand processional of all the graduates with the Junior high boys in suits and the girls in pastel colored outfits, the high school grads wore colored caps and gowns and the junior college grads wore regular black caps and gowns.

I started school at Alexander Hamilton Elementary School which was within walking distance from the house on Virginia Ave. It was a two storey building, not new and pretty standard looking for the time. It still stands at the corner of today (Oct. 2003).

A huge raised sandbox for Kindergartens only. Walking backwards one day and falling heels over head into the sandbox. The Kindergarten room being just an empty space, no desks, only a rug for sitting on and a separate area for cots where we had to rest. A little boy actually carving the leg off of a celluloid turkey. Making big structures out of blocks that did not have to be taken down at the end of the day.

The playground was divided into two parts, one on each side of the building. The "little side" for K thru 3rd grades was all asphalt, with a set of bars, a tetherball, and a backboard for bouncing balls against and playing some game where you threw the ball over the top. We played hopscotch, kick ball, the now forbidden game of dodge ball, in which the object was to throw or bounce a ball toward the other players lined up against the side of the building so as to hit them and get them "out". The girls played jump rope with two girls spinning a long rope and others jumping in and out of the rope to the rhythm of special chants. It was a game I never mastered, but I was good at marbles and played that with the boys. If you misbehaved during recess or lunch you were made to sit on the steps of the building where the teacher who was on "playground duty" kept her watchful eye. I found myself there one day, much to my chagrin, for some incident which I have forgotten. But I didn't forget the punishment. I was pretty much of a good kid so getting in trouble stuck with me. The only other time was during some kind of test, a state test or something, when I was falsely accused by the principal of looking at my neighbors work and humiliated by being publicly accused by him. I never forgave him for that and would confront him today were it possible.

One little boy, Harry, who was in my grade seemed to be often in trouble and was punished in ways that would not be tolerated today. I don't remember at all what he did but I do have vivid memories of the teachers taping his mouth shut, and recall that he often lost his recess privilege and was made to sit on a bench instead of playing and if he wouldn't stay put he was actually tied to the bench. When we were in the sixth grade he was sent to the office on some errand for the teacher and as soon as he came back in the room he shouted out "President Roosevelt is dead." The teachers first response was to assume he was being out of control, but in fact he was right, the President had died. That was April 12, 1945.

On the "big side" of the playground was a pergola, a covered area with long rows of tables and benches where we ate our lunch. There was also a cafeteria in a separate building and the line formed outside the building. I can't think what happened on rainy days.

## Junior High & High School

I was part of a very close knit group of teachers, who coached us, and students from all four grades who worked, played and socialized together. Eight different sports were offered during the year and we had intramural teams and also played other schools. Swimming was the only one I did not go out for, though I was sometimes a diving judge. The WAA had it's own large club room where members could study and eat lunch or just hang out. At the end of my first year I was chosen to be a member of the Women's Letter Club, a group chosen for their sports ability, participation and leadership, within the WAA. Every year I, along with other women, received a new level of WAA award and when I graduated was chosen to receive the highest award, a gold loving cup. This was not for being the best athlete, which I certainly was not, but for participation and contribution to the program. Because of being involved with WAA I was chosen to be part of two other honor groups, Spartans an all women's organization, and Order of Mast and Dagger the highest honor group, that included men and women from all kinds of interest groups who were giving service to the school in a variety of ways. When I graduated I was one of twenty seniors noted for their service to the school.

## UCLA

In 1954 I transferred to UCLA where I majored in health education with a minor in history. I joined Alpha Omicron Pi sorority and lived in the AOPI house on campus. Being in a sorority was a helpful thing, it provided a good and safe (an issue at UCLA at the time) place to live, an immediate group of friends and an abundant social life. Since I went as a junior I graduated after two years with a Bachelor of Science Degree. I was admitted to and began graduate school in the fall of 1955 to get my teaching credential, but at Christmas break became extremely ill with mononucleosis and missed the rest of the semester.

## JPL

By the time I was fully recovered from "mono" I had decided that I did not want to go back to school and went out to look for a job. I answered an ad from Caltech and was hired to work at the Jet Propulsion Lab in the Data Reduction group, an all women unit that did various kinds of math type support work for the engineers. This was the era just before computers took over all those functions and the women who worked in the group were actually called "computers." While in this group I was one of three people asked to participate in a "Secret Project" that was about to take place. It turned out to be the launching of Explorer I the United States first earth satellite.

## Mt. St. Helens

I was living in Southern California when Mt. St. Helen's erupted. It was a terrible, sad and interesting event but had no impact on my life. My sister and her family lived in Portland but other than ash fallout they were in no danger so I had no personal involvement.

## Family History

In the fall of 1887 my paternal grandfather graduated from McCormick Seminary and was given an appointment as a missionary to Japan by the Presbyterian Church. He was twenty nine years old and unmarried. A family friend suggested that he really should have a wife before he went off to Japan for five years and said she knew six young ladies had just graduated from a women's school that he should meet. One of those ladies was my grandmother and he decided she was the one he wanted to married and she agreed to that and they were married two months later and went off to Japan together.

My maternal grandmother was a very strong lady both in mind and body. One day she was driving a horse and buggy down the main street of Pasadena when the horse was spooked by something and started to run wildly down the road. My mother, who was a youngster at the time, was sitting beside her and she said her mother pulled so hard on the reins to get the horse under control that the horse actually sat down on the street and they were all safe.

Many of the women in my family, going back to my grandmothers, were bold and capable women in many different ways. I would like to see that trait passed on to the coming generations.
